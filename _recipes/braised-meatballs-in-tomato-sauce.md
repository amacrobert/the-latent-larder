---
title: Braised Meatballs in Tomato Sauce
date: 2026-07-27 08:45:50 -0400
description: Beef and pork meatballs browned hard, then finished low in a garlicky tomato sauce until a spoon is all you need to cut them.
image: /assets/images/Braised-Meatballs-In-Tomato-Sauce.webp
servings: 4
prep_time: 20 min
cook_time: 1 hr 15 min
tags: [beef, pork, main, slow]
ingredients:
  - 1 lb | ground beef
  - ½ lb | ground pork
  - ½ cup | dry breadcrumbs
  - ¼ cup | whole milk
  - 1 | egg
  - ½ cup | grated Parmesan
  - 2 cloves | garlic, minced
  - To taste | salt and pepper
  - 2 tbsp | olive oil
  - 1 | onion, diced
  - 3 cloves | garlic, sliced
  - 28 oz | canned whole tomatoes, crushed by hand
  - ½ cup | dry red wine
  - 1 sprig | basil
  - To finish | Parmesan and parsley
method:
  - Soak the breadcrumbs in the milk until soft, about 5 minutes.
  - Mix the soaked breadcrumbs with the beef, pork, egg, Parmesan, minced garlic, salt, and pepper by hand until just combined.
  - Roll into 16 meatballs, about 2 inches each.
  - Heat the olive oil in a heavy pot over medium-high and brown the meatballs on all sides, working in batches; transfer to a plate.
  - Add the onion and sliced garlic to the same pot and cook until softened, about 5 minutes.
  - Pour in the wine and scrape up the browned bits stuck to the bottom of the pot, then simmer until it's mostly reduced, about 3 minutes.
  - Add the crushed tomatoes and basil, bring to a simmer, and nestle the meatballs back into the sauce.
  - Cover partially and simmer on low until the meatballs are tender and the sauce has thickened, about 45 minutes.
  - Discard the basil sprig and taste the sauce for salt.
  - Top with Parmesan and parsley to serve.
---

Browning is for flavor here, not for cooking through — the meatballs finish in the sauce, which is what keeps them tender instead of dry. Milk-soaked breadcrumbs do the same job: they hold water in the mix so a lean cut like beef doesn't turn dense.

Good over polenta or a wide pasta, but also good with nothing but bread to drag through the sauce. Like most braises, it's better the next day once the sauce has had a night to settle into the meat.
