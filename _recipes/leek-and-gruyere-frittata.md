---
title: Leek and Gruyère Frittata
date: 2026-07-27 05:46:24 -0400
description: Eggs baked slow with sweet leeks and melted Gruyère until barely set — a breakfast that's just as good sliced cold at noon.
image: /assets/images/Leek-And-Gruyere-Frittata.webp
servings: 4
prep_time: 15 min
cook_time: 25 min
tags: [eggs, vegetarian, breakfast, baking]
ingredients:
  - 2 tbsp | butter
  - 2 | leeks, white and light green parts, thinly sliced
  - 8 | eggs
  - ½ cup | heavy cream
  - 1 cup | Gruyère, grated
  - ½ tsp | salt
  - Freshly ground pepper, to taste
  - A handful | chives, chopped, to finish
method:
  - Preheat the oven to 375°F and melt the butter in a 10-inch ovenproof skillet over medium heat.
  - Add the leeks and a pinch of salt, and cook, stirring occasionally, until they've collapsed and turned translucent, about 8 minutes.
  - Meanwhile, whisk the eggs, cream, and salt together in a large bowl until no streaks remain.
  - Stir half the Gruyère into the egg mixture, then pour it over the leeks in the skillet, tilting to distribute evenly.
  - Cook undisturbed on the stovetop until the edges just begin to set, about 2 minutes.
  - Scatter the remaining Gruyère over the top and transfer the skillet to the oven.
  - Bake until the center is set but still barely jiggles and the top is puffed and golden in spots, 15 to 18 minutes.
  - Let rest for 5 minutes, then scatter with chives and cut into wedges.
---

Pull it while the center still has a slight wobble — it keeps cooking in the residual heat of the pan, and a frittata baked until fully firm before resting turns rubbery by the time it hits the table. The wide surface area of a wide skillet also matters more than you'd think: crowd the eggs into a smaller pot and the center takes so long to set that the edges turn tough waiting for it.

Swap the leeks for a bunch of scallions or a handful of sautéed spinach if that's what's around, and Gruyère for any cheese that melts well — cheddar or fontina both work. It's built for breakfast but sits at room temperature for hours without suffering, which makes it as much a brunch-table dish as a weekday one.
