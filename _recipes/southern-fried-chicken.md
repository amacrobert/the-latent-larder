---
title: Southern Buttermilk Fried Chicken
date: 2026-08-11 06:00:02 -0400
description: Bone-in chicken brined overnight in buttermilk, dredged heavy, and shallow-fried in a cast-iron skillet until the crust cracks like glass.
image: /assets/images/Southern-Fried-Chicken.webp
servings: 4
prep_time: 20 min (plus overnight brine)
cook_time: 35 min
tags: [chicken, fried, main]
ingredients:
  - 3½ lb | bone-in, skin-on chicken thighs and drumsticks
  - 2 cups | buttermilk
  - 1 tbsp | hot sauce
  - 1 tbsp | kosher salt, divided
  - 2 cups | all-purpose flour
  - 1 tbsp | paprika
  - 2 tsp | garlic powder
  - 2 tsp | onion powder
  - 1 tsp | cayenne
  - 1 tsp | black pepper
  - 1 tsp | baking powder
  - For frying | peanut or vegetable oil
method:
  - Whisk the buttermilk, hot sauce, and half the salt together, submerge the chicken pieces, and refrigerate at least 4 hours, preferably overnight.
  - Whisk the flour, paprika, garlic powder, onion powder, cayenne, pepper, baking powder, and the remaining salt in a wide dish.
  - Lift each piece from the buttermilk, letting the excess drip off, then press it firmly and repeatedly into the flour until heavily coated and craggy on every side.
  - Set the floured chicken on a rack and let it sit 10 minutes so the crust sets and won't slide off in the oil.
  - Pour oil into a heavy pot or cast-iron skillet to a depth of 1½ inches and heat to 325°F.
  - Fry the chicken skin-side down in batches, adjusting the heat to hold between 300°F and 325°F, until deep mahogany brown, 12 to 15 minutes, then flip and fry until a thermometer reads 165°F near the bone, another 12 to 15 minutes.
  - Drain on a wire rack, not paper towels, and let rest 5 minutes before serving.
---

The overnight buttermilk soak is doing two jobs at once: tenderizing the meat and giving the flour something to grip. Press the chicken into the flour rather than just turning it over — those raised, craggy edges are what actually go shatter-crisp, and a smooth dredge fries up smooth and soft. Bone-in pieces need a real thermometer check near the bone, since the surface will look done well before the meat is.

Keep the oil temperature honest — it drops the moment cold chicken goes in, and if it doesn't climb back toward 300°F within a minute or two, the crust will drink oil instead of crisping. A splatter screen and a spare pair of tongs make this a one-person job.
